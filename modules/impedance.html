Module 02  ·  Single-trace attributes

Relative acoustic impedance: turning edges back into layers

A seismic trace does not record rock. It records the places where rock changes. That is why a thick uniform sandstone shows up as two wiggles and nothing in between, and why a wiggle display is such an awkward thing to hand a geologist. This module runs the change backward. One step of arithmetic — adding the trace up as you go down it — turns a record of boundaries into something shaped like a record of layers. The rest of the module is about what that step quietly assumes.

1Seismic records edges, not layers

Where the trace comes from in the first place

Start with the rock. Acoustic impedance is a property of a layer: its velocity multiplied by its density. Every rock has one, and it stays the same all the way through a uniform bed. The left-hand panel below is the impedance of the rocks under the trace you have selected. It looks like a set of steps, because that is what layered rock is.

A seismic wave does not care about impedance. It cares about changes in impedance. At every boundary a fraction of the wave bounces back, and that fraction is the reflection coefficient:

r = (Zbelow − Zabove) / (Zbelow + Zabove)

The middle panel is that calculation applied to the log on the left. It is empty wherever the rock is uniform, and it spikes at each boundary. Then the earth blurs those spikes with a wavelet, and you get the trace on the right.

Look at what happened to the thick limestone near the top. Two boundaries produced two spikes and the trace shows two events. The layer itself left no mark at all. That is the whole problem this module is about: a geologist wants to know about the layer, and the trace is telling them about its edges.

The impedance numbers here are in the usual units of 106 kg m−2 s−1, which is velocity times density, and typical sedimentary rocks land between about 4 and 15. Notice the slow rise in the background level with depth. That is compaction: rocks get faster and denser as you bury them. Hold on to it, because in step 4 it goes missing.

2Run it backward

Adding the trace up as you go down it

Going from the impedance log to the trace, the operation that mattered was differencing: each spike came from the change across a boundary. The way back is the opposite operation, integration — running down the trace and keeping a running total. Every time the trace has a positive bump the total steps up and stays up. Between events the trace is near zero, so the total holds still. Steps in, steps out.

There is a tidy piece of algebra underneath. For small reflection coefficients,

r  ≈  ½ × the change in ln(Z)

so a trace of reflection coefficients is very nearly the derivative of ½ln(Z), and integrating it hands back ½ln(Z) itself. That is what the middle panel is. The right-hand panel is the true impedance log from step 1, put through the same filter the seismic went through, so the two can be compared fairly.

The readout below measures how close they are. On the default settings the two agree to a correlation of —, which is close enough that you can read the integrated trace as a layer property rather than a boundary marker. That is the entire attribute.

Compare the two sections on the right. The same information is in both, but they ask different things of you. On the amplitude section you read the lines; on the impedance section you read the blocks between them. For picking the top of a reservoir the amplitude display is better, because the event is sharp. For seeing that the reservoir is thicker here than there, the impedance display wins, because a thick low-impedance body looks like a thick low-impedance body.

3Everything moves a quarter of a cycle

The mistake that puts your pick in the wrong place

Integration does something to the wavelet that you have to know about before picking anything. Dividing a spectrum by frequency rotates the phase by 90 degrees. In plain terms: peaks stop being peaks. Where the trace had a bright peak at a boundary, the integrated version has a step at that boundary, and its own maximum sits in the middle of the layer below.

The panels below zoom in on one event so you can see the two curves side by side. The boundary is marked with a dashed line. The trace peaks on it. The integrated trace is climbing through it, at its steepest, and does not reach its own maximum until — later.

So the rule for reading these sections is different, and it catches people out constantly. On an amplitude section, the boundary is the bright line. On a relative impedance section, the boundary is where the color changes. Pick the bright part of an impedance display and you have picked the middle of the layer.

4What the word "relative" is doing

The part of the answer that cannot come back

In step 1 the impedance log had a slow rise from top to bottom, the compaction trend. Find it on the integrated trace below. It is not there.

This is not a flaw in the arithmetic, and no better algorithm would fix it. A slow trend is a very low frequency, well under 1 Hz, and seismic data does not record frequencies that low. The trend never made it into the trace, so integration has nothing to give back. The seismic simply never knew.

The consequence is the whole reason for the word relative in the name:

  • There is no absolute scale. The numbers on an impedance display are not impedance. You cannot look one up and say the layer is 9.2. Values are only meaningful against their neighbors.
  • You cannot compare things that are far apart. Two beds of genuinely identical impedance, one shallow and one deep, will read differently, because each is floating relative to whatever surrounds it.
  • The zero level means nothing. It is wherever the arithmetic left it.

Move the low-frequency slider in the header and watch the third panel. As you let the data record lower and lower frequencies, more of the trend comes back and the integrated version gets closer to the truth. That slider is the single most important control on this page, and in real life it is not a slider — it is whatever your acquisition and processing happened to deliver.

Real seismic inversion closes this gap by bringing the missing low frequencies in from somewhere else, usually well logs and a velocity model. This attribute does not do that, which is what makes it an attribute rather than an inversion.

5The filter that stops it running away

Why there is a bandpass at the end of the flow

Integration in the frequency domain means dividing by frequency. That is fine in the middle of the seismic band. Near zero frequency it is not fine at all, because dividing by a number approaching zero produces a number approaching infinity. Anything down there — a little noise, a small offset left over from processing — gets multiplied enormously.

You can see it. The filter corner slider below sets the lowest frequency the attribute is allowed to keep. Take it to 0 Hz, which switches the filter off, and turn the noise up in the header. The result wanders off the scale in long slow swings that have nothing to do with geology. This is why AASPI's relative_acoustic_impedance applies a four-point Ormsby bandpass after the integration, and why that filter is not optional.

Setting the corner is a judgement call. Too low and the noise comes through. Too high and you throw away the low frequencies that made the attribute worth computing, since the whole point was to emphasize them. Somewhere just below the lowest frequency your data actually recorded is the usual answer.

One detail worth knowing if you are comparing this against AASPI. In this module the wavelet has already band-limited the trace, so the filter is only removing what falls outside a band the data never occupied. On real data the low end is full of ground roll, swell noise and residual statics, and the filter is doing far more work than it appears to be doing here. The AASPI documentation also recommends running spectral balancing first, so that the wavelet is closer to a spike before any of this happens.

?Why anyone bothers

What it is good for, and what it is not

Trace integration is about as simple as a seismic attribute gets, and it survives because it changes the question the display answers. An amplitude section asks you to interpret lines. An impedance section asks you to interpret bodies. Geologists think in bodies.

What it is used for

  • Seeing the shape of a body. A channel, a bar, a reef or a sand package reads as a blob with a top and a base rather than as two separate reflections you have to mentally join up.
  • Tying to wells. An impedance log from sonic and density can be plotted straight onto an impedance section. Comparing a log against a wiggle display is a much harder piece of visual work.
  • Stratigraphic interpretation. Onlap, downlap and truncation are easier to see when layers look like layers, and the quarter-cycle rotation makes bed boundaries fall where the color changes.
  • A first look before committing to inversion. Proper inversion needs wells, a wavelet estimate and a low-frequency model. This needs a button. If the relative version shows nothing, the expensive version will probably not rescue it.

The four ways it will mislead you

It is not inversion, and the difference is not a technicality. Inversion brings in the low frequencies that seismic never recorded, from wells and velocities, and removes the wavelet. This does neither. It is the seismic trace with its phase rotated and its low end boosted. Anyone who reads absolute rock properties off it is reading something that is not there.

Bright is not the boundary. The quarter-cycle rotation from step 3 moves every event, and a pick made by habit on the bright part of the display lands in the middle of the layer instead of on its top.

It assumes the reflection coefficients are small. The step from r to ½ ln(Z) is an approximation that holds beautifully at a shale-on-shale contact and starts to slip at a hard limestone or a salt boundary. Push the contrast slider to 3 and the integrated result under-reads the true impedance by a measurable amount, always in the same direction.

It assumes the wavelet is close to a spike. The AASPI documentation says so directly, and recommends spectral balancing first. Turn the phase slider in the header and watch the match with the true log: 0.997 at zero phase, 0.884 at 30°, 0.530 at 60°, and 0.031 at 90°, where the attribute and the rock have almost nothing left in common. At 180° it reads −0.997. That is not a small error. An impedance display made from reverse-polarity data is a near-perfect mirror of the truth, and every hard bed on it looks soft. Nothing on the screen tells you this has happened.

Worth carrying: this attribute makes the data easier to look at without adding anything to it. Everything on the impedance display was already in the trace. That is a genuine and useful thing to do, and it is a smaller claim than the display makes it look.

Exercises

  1. Count boundaries and count events. In step 1, look at the thick limestone at the top of the log and count how many boundaries it has, then count the events the trace shows there.
    HintThe limestone has two boundaries. Its top is at 158 ms, where impedance jumps from 6.13 to 8.81 and r = +0.179. Its base is at 214 ms, where it drops back and r = −0.177. Two boundaries, two events, and 56 ms of rock between them that produced neither. The trace is not quite flat in between — it reaches 0.39 there — but that is the tails of the two wavelets overlapping, not a response from the limestone itself. Nothing about the inside of the bed reached the recording, because nothing inside it changes. This is why a wiggle display is an awkward thing to hand a geologist: they want to know about the 56 ms, and the trace is telling them about its two edges.
  2. Find the trend that went missing. In step 4, read the true impedance at the top of the section and at the bottom, then read the integrated trace at the same two places.
    HintTrue impedance runs from 5.99 at the top of the section to 9.95 at the bottom, a factor of 1.66. At those same two places the attribute reads 0.121 and −0.038, so it does not merely miss the rise, it drifts the other way. Two numbers pin it down: matched against the true log the correlation is 0.434, and matched against the same log with the trend removed it is 0.997. The attribute does a nearly perfect job of the part it can do and none at all of the part it cannot. If you want the trend, you have to bring it from somewhere else, which is what inversion does and this does not.
  3. Buy the low frequencies back. In step 4, move the lowest-recorded-frequency slider from 20 Hz down to 2 Hz, watching the two match numbers.
    HintReading the first number as you go: 20 Hz gives 0.245, then 0.270 at 16, 0.342 at 12, 0.434 at 8, 0.494 at 6 and 0.556 at 4. Every hertz recorded at the low end buys back a little more of the trend, and it never gets close to 1 — you would have to record down to a fraction of a hertz for that, and nobody does. Watch the second number as well. It sits near 0.99 most of the way and then falls to 0.948 at 4 Hz. That is not the attribute failing. That is the filter from step 5, still sitting at 5 Hz, throwing away the 4 Hz signal you just paid to record. The two settings have to be chosen with each other in mind.
  4. Break the small-contrast assumption. In step 2, set the impedance contrast to 0.3, note the match with the true log, then take it to 3.0 and look again.
    HintAt ×0.3 the largest reflection coefficient on the trace is 0.055 and the integrated result recovers essentially 100% of the log impedance. At ×1 the largest r is 0.179 and it recovers 99.4%. At ×3, r reaches 0.494 and it recovers 94.1%. The shortfall always goes the same way, because r = tanh(½ΔlnZ) is always slightly smaller than ½ΔlnZ, and integration returns the r it was given. Now watch the correlation while you do it: 0.997 at every setting. The shape does not degrade at all; only the size is wrong. That is a comfortable failure to live with while you are ranking layers against each other, and a dangerous one if you start quoting numbers.
  5. Switch the filter off. In step 5, put the noise at 20% in the header, then take the filter corner from 5 Hz down to 0.
    HintAt 20% noise with the corner at 5 Hz the largest excursion on the attribute is 0.93 and the match with the true log is 0.992. Take the corner to 0 and the excursion doubles to 1.90 while the match collapses to 0.452. At 40% noise it is 3.15 and 0.234. That extra size is not geology; it is noise sitting below the seismic band, divided by a very small frequency. Then try the opposite mistake and set the corner to 12 Hz. The drift is gone, but the match drops to 0.835, because now you are filtering out the low frequencies of the data itself. Both failures look like a smooth, plausible section. Neither announces itself.

The main points

  • Seismic records boundaries; integration gives back layers. Reflection coefficients are close to the derivative of ½ln(impedance), so adding the trace up as you go down it returns something shaped like the impedance log.
  • Relative means relative. The trend with depth is a frequency too low for seismic to record, so it cannot come back. There is no absolute scale and no meaningful zero, and two layers far apart cannot be compared.
  • Everything shifts by a quarter of a cycle. Bed boundaries land where the color changes, not where the display is brightest. Picking by brightness puts you in the middle of the layer.
  • The bandpass at the end is load-bearing. Dividing by frequency amplifies whatever sits near zero frequency without limit, and the filter is what stops the section drifting.
  • Two assumptions come with it. Small reflection coefficients, and a wavelet close to a spike. Both are stated in the AASPI documentation, and both are visible on the sliders here.
  • It is not inversion. No wavelet has been removed and no low-frequency model has been added. It is a re-display of the trace, and a good one.
Method, sources, and what has been simplified

The data. The model is a set of impedance layers rather than a set of reflection coefficients, which matters here because the module compares the attribute against a truth that has to exist independently of it. Impedance is built as a compaction trend, rising from about 5.6 to 9.0 × 106 kg m−2 s−1 over one second, with layers imposed on it as departures in ln(Z): a limestone, a soft shale, a thinning hard bed, a sand that is gas-filled on one side of the line and brine-filled on the other, a package of interbeds, a channel fill, and a deep hard streak, all draped over a low-relief anticline. The contrast slider multiplies every departure in the log domain, which is what makes it a clean test of the small-reflection-coefficient assumption.

Reflection coefficients come from the impedances by r = (Z₂ − Z₁)/(Z₂ + Z₁) at each boundary, and the trace is those coefficients convolved with an Ormsby wavelet whose low corner is the slider in the header and whose high corner is fixed at 45 and 55 Hz. The compaction trend produces no reflection of its own, which is not an omission: a gradient that slow is far below the seismic band, and a real survey would not record it either. That is precisely why it cannot be recovered in step 4.

The attribute follows AASPI's relative_acoustic_impedance: the trace is transformed, divided by iω, multiplied by a four-point Ormsby filter, and transformed back. The filter is a trapezoid, linear on its ramps, with its low corner set by the slider in step 5 and its high corner well above the data. Zero frequency and Nyquist are set to zero, the first because an integral cannot recover a constant and the second so the result stays real.

What the truth panel shows. The comparison curve is ½ln(Z) of the model, filtered with the same Ormsby response, so that the attribute is not blamed for frequencies the seismic never had. Correlations quoted in the readouts are Pearson correlations over the drawn time window. The integrated trace and the true log are drawn on separate axes rather than overlaid, because the attribute carries the wavelet and the log does not, so their absolute sizes differ by a factor that has nothing to teach anyone. What is comparable is the shape, and that is what the correlation measures.

Where the approximation sits. Exactly, r = tanh(½ Δln Z), so integrating reflection coefficients returns slightly less than ½ln(Z), and the shortfall grows with contrast. Measured on this model with the same code that draws the panels: at the smallest contrast setting the integrated result is 100.0% of the true log impedance, at the default 99.4%, and at the largest 94.1%. The shape survives throughout — the correlation stays above 0.999 — so the error is one of size, not of position.

Simplifications, and where this departs from AASPI. This is one dimension: normal incidence, acoustic, no multiples, no transmission loss, no offset, and a wavelet that does not change with depth. AASPI runs on a 3D volume and recommends spectral_balance beforehand to bring the wavelet closer to the spike the method assumes; there is no equivalent step here, and the phase slider is the closest this module comes to showing what a non-ideal wavelet costs. The Ormsby ramps here are linear in amplitude; production implementations often taper them, which changes the ringing slightly and nothing else.
