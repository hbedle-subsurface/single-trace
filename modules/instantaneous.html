Module 01  ·  Single-trace attributes

Instantaneous attributes: the trace as a spinning arrow

A seismic trace is one number per sample. That is not much to work with, so we make a second trace out of the first one — the same wiggle, shifted a quarter of a cycle — and read the two together as an arrow going round and round. Everything in this module is then one of three questions about that arrow: how long is it (envelope), where in the turn is it (phase), and how fast is it turning (frequency).

1One trace, and the trace you never see

The Hilbert transform, without the equation

Pick any trace out of the line above and you have a single wiggly curve: one amplitude value every two milliseconds. Ask it how strong the reflection is at a given moment and you hit a problem immediately. Halfway between a peak and a trough the trace passes through zero. The reflection is not weak there. The curve just happens to be crossing the axis on its way somewhere.

Here is the fix, and it is the whole trick behind every attribute in this module. Alongside the recorded trace we build a second one, called the Hilbert transform. It contains exactly the same frequencies with exactly the same strengths — it is only shifted by a quarter of a cycle, so that where the original has a peak, the shifted one crosses zero. It is what you would have recorded if every wave had left the source a quarter cycle earlier.

Think of a bicycle wheel with a reflector clipped to the rim, seen edge on in the dark. All you see is a dot going up and down — that is the trace. Seen from the side at the same moment you would see it going side to side instead, a quarter turn out of step. Two shadows, one wheel. Put the two together and you get the wheel back: the position of the reflector at every instant. That recovered arrow is the complex trace, or analytic trace, A(t) = u(t) + i·uH(t).

2Envelope: how strong, wherever you look

The length of the arrow

The length of the arrow is the envelope, also called reflection strength or instantaneous amplitude: e(t) = √(u² + uH²). It is the outline the wiggle fits inside. Three things follow from that definition, and all three are visible below.

  • It is never negative. Squares and a square root. A trough is just as strong a reflection as a peak, and the envelope says so — it has thrown polarity away.
  • It does not care about phase. Rotate the wavelet with the slider above and the wiggle changes completely: peaks become troughs, zero crossings land somewhere new. The envelope hardly moves.
  • It is smooth. One event gives one envelope hump, not a peak and two side lobes. That is why the envelope is the attribute to use for picking where something is, and the raw trace is the one to use for picking exactly when.

3Phase: where in the wiggle you are

The angle of the arrow, and why it is drawn as a cosine

The angle of the arrow is the instantaneous phase, φ(t) = atan2(uH, u). It says where you are within the wavelet and nothing about how strong that wavelet is: 0° at a peak, ±180° at a trough, ±90° at the zero crossings between them. Divide the trace by its own envelope and every event, strong or weak, comes out the same size. That is the attribute's whole personality — phase has no amplitude in it at all.

Which makes it very good at one job. Turn the amplitude decay up and watch the deep reflectors fade out of the amplitude section. On the phase section they do not fade, because there is nothing left to fade. A reflector that is too weak to see can still be followed.

There is a catch, and it is the reason nobody displays raw phase. An angle wraps: as it passes 180° it reappears at −180°, so the phase curve is a sawtooth full of cliffs that are not in the data. The usual fix is to plot the cosine of the phase instead. It carries the same information, it goes smoothly from +1 at a peak to −1 at a trough, and it has no jumps.

4Frequency: how fast the arrow turns

The most misused attribute in the list

If phase is the angle, instantaneous frequency is how fast that angle is changing: f(t) = (1/2π) dφ/dt. Where the wiggles are close together the arrow spins quickly and the frequency is high; where they are stretched out it is low. Computed properly it is never differenced from the wrapped sawtooth — that would put a spike at every wrap — but from the traces themselves, which is what the panel below does.

Now look at what it does between events. The instantaneous frequency runs off the scale and goes negative, which as a description of a seismic wavelet is meaningless. It is not a bug and it is not noise. Where two events overlap, the arrow passes close to the origin, and an arrow near the origin can swing through a large angle in one sample while barely moving. The frequency is a derivative of an angle that is not well defined there.

So nobody ships the raw version alone. There are two standard repairs and the panel shows both:

  • Weighted-average frequency. Average over a short window, weighting every sample by its energy, so the unstable low-energy samples contribute almost nothing. The window length is yours to choose — try the slider.
  • Wavelet frequency, also called response frequency. Find each envelope peak, read the frequency there, and hold that one value across the whole event. It is blocky on purpose: one number per reflection, measured where the reflection is strongest.

Instantaneous frequency is a mean of everything present at that instant, weighted by energy; the dominant frequency is the peak of a spectrum. They are different quantities that happen to share a name and a unit. Comparing an instantaneous frequency map against a spectral-decomposition peak-frequency map without knowing that will cost you an afternoon.

5Sweetness: two attributes in a trench coat

Bright and low frequency, in one number

Radovich and Oliveros noticed in the Gulf of Mexico that sands encased in shale tended to show up two ways at once: higher envelope and lower frequency than the shale around them. Rather than display two maps, they built one attribute that goes up when both happen together: sweetness = e / √f.

That formula is the easy part. What decides how the map looks is which envelope and which frequency you put into it, because instantaneous frequency on its own is far too unstable to divide by — step 4 showed why. Two answers are in use, and the toggle below switches between them. Radovich and Oliveros used the response attributes: one value per event, held between envelope minima, which makes the result a staircase. What interpretation packages actually display is the smooth version, built from the envelope itself over an energy-weighted average frequency. AASPI reaches the same smooth place from the other direction, by running a median filter over the response version. Switch between the two and watch what happens: the value at each event barely moves, and everything between the events changes completely.

The line above has a gas sand in it, on the right-hand side between about 0.47 and 0.52 s. It is brighter than its brine-filled equivalent on the left, and it is lower frequency, because gas attenuates the high frequencies passing through it. Both effects push sweetness the same way, so the sand stands out on the sweetness section more than on either input.

And then the caution, which matters more than the attribute. Sweetness has no units that anyone can defend — an amplitude divided by the square root of a frequency. Its absolute value means nothing at all between two surveys, or between two versions of the same survey processed with different gain. It is a ranking within one volume, and it is only as good as the amplitudes going in. Anything that brightens the data brightens sweetness, whether it is a sand or not.

That is the entire case for the attribute. It is also the entire risk: a bright spot with no frequency change, or a low-frequency zone with no brightening, will still raise sweetness, and the map cannot tell you which of the two ingredients did it. Notice also that the two stabilization modes give nearly the same number at the sand — 0.164 against 0.162 — because at an envelope peak the response value and the averaged value are read off the same wavelet. Their disagreement is everywhere else, which is most of the volume.

?Why these attributes exist

What they are used for, and how they mislead

These are the oldest seismic attributes there are — Taner, Koehler and Sheriff published the complex trace method in 1979 — and they are still the ones you meet first, because they cost almost nothing to compute and they are on every interpretation system by default. That availability is exactly why they get misread.

What they are used for

  • Envelope, for finding things. Bright spots, channel fills, tuned thin beds and any place where reflection strength is the message. It is also the standard input to a first-pass direct-hydrocarbon-indicator screen, because it does not depend on knowing the phase of the processed data.
  • Cosine of phase, for following things. Tracking a weak reflector through a dim zone, picking horizons across an amplitude change, and seeing onlap and truncation geometry where the amplitude display is dominated by a few strong beds.
  • Frequency, for thickness and for gas. Low-frequency shadows under gas-charged sands, tuning effects in thin beds, and attenuation in general. Almost always as a wavelet or averaged frequency, rarely as the raw instantaneous value.
  • Sweetness, as a screening map. A quick first look at where the bright, low-frequency packages are in a new volume, before anything more careful is done.
  • All of them as inputs to something else. Envelope and frequency go into machine-learning facies classification and into multi-attribute displays far more often than they are interpreted on their own.

The four ways they will mislead you

An envelope is not a reflection coefficient. Two events half a wavelet apart give one envelope hump, not two, and the height of that hump depends on how they interfere as much as on how strong either one is. The wedge in the middle of this line does exactly that. Its two reflection coefficients are fixed at ±0.085 from one end to the other, and yet at the default settings the peak envelope across it runs 0.53 where the bed is 40 ms thick, 0.85 at 14 ms, and 0.18 at 2 ms — a factor of nearly five, all of it interference. An envelope map of a thinning sand is a map of its thickness at least as much as of its impedance contrast.

Raw instantaneous frequency is not the frequency of anything. It is defined sample by sample, and it goes negative where events overlap: 2.3% of the samples on the default trace, and 8.6% of them once the dominant frequency is raised to 40 Hz. Whenever you are handed a "frequency" volume, ask which of the three it is: the instantaneous one, an energy-weighted average over a window, or the wavelet value held across each event. They can differ by tens of hertz at the same sample.

Phase has thrown the amplitude away on purpose. That is what makes it useful, and it also means noise gets the same treatment as signal. In a quiet zone with nothing in it, the cosine of phase will happily paint confident-looking bands out of whatever is there. Turn the noise slider up and look at the deep part of the phase section for the full effect.

Every one of them is amplitude-dependent, whatever the marketing says. Envelope and sweetness scale directly with the amplitudes going in, so a gain change, a different processing vintage or an AGC applied earlier in the flow will change their values everywhere. That is the subject of modules 03 and 04.

The habit worth building: before interpreting any of these, ask what was done to the amplitudes upstream. An envelope map made from AGC'd data is a map of the AGC window as much as of the geology.

Exercises

  1. Prove the envelope ignores phase. In step 2, note where the trace peaks and where the envelope peaks on the strongest event. Now take the wavelet phase slider from 0° to 90° and read both again.
    HintAt 0° the trace peaks at 158 ms and so does the envelope. At 45° the trace peak has moved to 162 ms, at 90° to 166 ms, and at 135° the largest excursion has become a trough at 154 ms. By 180° the event is a trough at 158 ms. The envelope peak does not move at any of those settings, and its value stays 0.896 throughout. Two things follow. The envelope is the honest answer to "where is the reflection", because it is the only one of the two that does not depend on a processing decision. And if you need the time of a reflection to the millisecond, you need to know the phase of your data first, because the wiggle will give you a different answer every time that phase changes.
  2. Follow a reflector that is not there. In step 3, look at the reflector at 0.77 s, which fades out toward the middle of the line. Compare it on the amplitude section and on the cosine-of-phase section, then push the amplitude decay to 24 dB/s and look again.
    HintAt the default 6 dB/s the envelope of that reflector reads 0.434 on a bright trace and 0.031 where it has faded — a factor of fourteen — while the cosine of phase reads 1.00 and 0.95, essentially the same event in both places. Push the decay to 24 dB/s and the envelopes become 0.091 and 0.006, still a factor of fourteen, and cos φ still reads 1.00 and 0.86. Nothing has been recovered that was not recorded; the reflector was always there, and dividing by the envelope simply stops the strong events from owning the display. Now turn the noise to 30%. The envelope ratio apparently improves to ×0.28, which is the noise filling in rather than the reflector returning, and cos φ on the faded trace flips to −0.48. That is the warning that comes with the attribute: once there is no amplitude left to divide by, phase describes the noise exactly as confidently as it described the reflector.
  3. Make instantaneous frequency misbehave. In step 4, read the range of the instantaneous frequency and the fraction of samples that come out negative, then move to a trace in the thin part of the wedge, around trace 80.
    HintOn the default trace at 25 Hz the instantaneous frequency runs from −20 to 41 Hz, and 10 of the 441 samples in the window come out negative — 2.3%. Raise the dominant frequency to 40 Hz and the range opens to −386 to 45 Hz with 8.6% negative. Move to trace 20, where the wedge is at its thickest and the two reflections only partly overlap, and it is −200 to 39 Hz with 6.1%. Notice which places are worst. It is not where the two events merge: at trace 80 the bed is 6 ms thick, the two reflections have fused into one clean event, and the range is only −87 to 41 Hz. It is where they partly overlap, so that the arrow swings close to the origin. That is also why raising the dominant frequency makes it worse — a shorter wavelet leaves more low-energy gaps between events.
  4. Choose an averaging window and see what it costs. In step 4, put the averaging window at ±4 ms, then at ±60 ms, watching the frequency curve and the gas sand at 0.47 s.
    HintPut the cursor at 300 ms on the default trace, in the low-energy gap above the wedge, and read the three numbers with the window at ±16 ms: instantaneous 0.1 Hz, weighted average 2.3 Hz, wavelet 28.2 Hz. Same sample, same trace, three attributes that are all called frequency, disagreeing by a factor of nearly 300. Now sweep the window. At ±4 ms the average reads 0.2 Hz, because it is still local enough to be dominated by the same dead zone. At ±60 ms it reads 27.3 Hz, because the window now reaches the events on either side. Nothing about the data changed between those two readings. The conclusion is not that one of the three is correct; it is that a frequency volume is a choice, and that the choice has to travel with the volume or the next person will compare it against something else entirely.
  5. Take the sweetness apart. In step 5, read the envelope, frequency and sweetness of the gas sand and of the brine sand, and work out how much of the contrast comes from each ingredient.
    HintGas sand: envelope 0.652, average frequency 15.8 Hz, sweetness 0.164. Brine sand: 0.198, 27.7 Hz, 0.038. The envelope on its own separates them by ×3.29. The frequency drop contributes √(27.7 / 15.8) = ×1.32. Multiply the two and you get ×4.36, which is the ×4.35 sweetness ratio to within rounding — sweetness is exactly its two ingredients and nothing else. So on this model a little under a quarter of the contrast comes from the frequency term and the rest from brightness. Worth knowing before crediting a sweetness anomaly to gas: a bright spot with no frequency change at all would have produced most of this anomaly on its own. Switch the toggle to the response attributes and the two ratios are ×4.33 and ×3.29 — the same conclusion, from a map that looks quite different.
  6. Make the case for sweetness in one number. In step 6, compare how well the envelope map and the sweetness map separate the gas lobe from the brine sand beside it, then slide the window down past the sand.
    HintOver the sand, at 0 to 60 ms below the horizon, the envelope map reads 0.511 inside the lobe against 0.129 outside it, a factor of 3.96. The sweetness map reads 0.143 against 0.025, a factor of 5.78 — about ×1.5 more separation from the same traces, which is the frequency term from step 5 arriving as extra contrast. Slide the window to 80 to 120 ms and both collapse, to ×1.46 and ×2.09, because you are no longer looking at the sand at all. Notice that sweetness keeps more of its contrast even there, which is not always a good thing: an attribute that responds to something in every window is an attribute that will find you an anomaly wherever you put the window.

The main points

  • Every attribute here comes from one arrow. The recorded trace and its quarter-cycle shift are two views of the same rotating vector. Its length is the envelope, its angle is the phase, and the rate the angle changes is the frequency. Nothing else is going on.
  • The envelope is strength with polarity and phase removed. Never negative, one hump per event, and it stays put when the wavelet is rotated — which is why it is good for finding features and poor for timing them.
  • Phase is position within the wavelet, with amplitude divided out. That is why it follows weak reflectors through dim zones, and why it will paint convincing structure out of noise.
  • Raw instantaneous frequency is unstable by construction. It goes negative where events interfere, because the angle it differentiates is undefined when the arrow passes near the origin.
  • There are three different "frequency" attributes. Instantaneous, energy-weighted average, and wavelet or response frequency. Always find out which one you have been given.
  • These are map attributes. Sections teach you what they do; envelope and sweetness maps over an interval are what they are used for, and the gas lobe has an edge that no section can show as an edge.
  • Sweetness is a ranking, not a measurement. Envelope over the square root of frequency, with no defensible units, no meaning across surveys, and a direct dependence on whatever gain was applied upstream.
Method, sources, and what has been simplified

The data. The section is a synthetic: 96 traces of 500 samples at a 2 ms sample interval, built by convolving a Ricker wavelet with a set of reflection coefficients. It contains a shallow marker, a thinning wedge, a gas sand with a brine-filled equivalent along strike, a package of weak interbeds, a reflector that dims laterally, and a deep marker, all draped over a low-relief anticline. The gas sand and everything below it inside the gas window are convolved with a wavelet at 62% of the dominant frequency, which is a crude but honest way to produce the attenuation a gas sand really causes. Amplitude decay is applied as a gain function in dB per second; noise is band-limited to the wavelet and expressed as a percentage of the section's RMS amplitude. Everything is normalized by a single fixed constant taken from the default settings, so that changing a slider changes the numbers you see rather than silently rescaling the axes.

The Hilbert transform is computed in the frequency domain, as the AASPI documentation describes: transform, zero the negative frequencies and double the positive ones, transform back, and take the imaginary part. The time-domain sum over reciprocal odd integers gives the same answer but has to be truncated. The sign convention used throughout is uH = sin(ωt) when u = cos(ωt), so that the phase increases with time and a normal event has a positive frequency. Getting that sign backwards negates every frequency in a volume without changing the envelope at all, which is a hard error to notice.

Envelope, phase and frequency follow Taner et al. (1979): e = √(u² + uH²), φ = atan2(uH, u), and for the frequency the chain-rule form f = (1/2π)(u·duH/dt − uH·du/dt) / e² rather than a difference of the wrapped phase. The two derivatives are taken through the Fourier transform, again following the documentation. No stabilization is applied to the denominator, deliberately: where the envelope goes to zero the answer really is unstable, and hiding that would hide the reason the other two frequency attributes exist.

Weighted-average frequency follows Barnes (2000, 2016) as AASPI implements it: the instantaneous frequency averaged over a window with each sample weighted by its instantaneous power e². The window here is a raised cosine. The same weights give the RMS frequency and hence σ² = fRMS² − favg², from which AASPI reports a full bandwidth of 2σ. That output is computed here but not displayed, and it is worth saying why: on an isolated wavelet this formula returns a very small number, because the instantaneous frequency barely varies across a single clean event. It measures the spread of instantaneous frequency inside the window, which equals the spectral bandwidth only when the envelope is not also changing — Barnes's instantaneous bandwidth term |de/dt| / 2πe carries the rest. Read an average-bandwidth volume as a measure of how mixed the frequency content is, not as the width of a spectrum.

Wavelet attributes follow Bodine (1984). Local envelope maxima and minima are found, and the phase and frequency at each maximum are held across the interval between the minima either side. The result is blocky by construction. AASPI offers Barnes's weighted alternative as well, which is the averaged version above.

Sweetness is e / √f after Radovich and Oliveros (1998). The formula is not in dispute; the inputs are. Their paper, and the AASPI documentation following it, use the response attributes, which are piecewise constant by construction — taken literally that produces a staircase, and no package displays sweetness that way. The AASPI GUI resolves it with a user-defined median filter over the result. This module instead offers both inputs explicitly and defaults to the smooth one: the envelope over the square root of the energy-weighted average frequency, which is Barnes's stabilization rather than Bodine's and is what a sweetness volume looks like in practice. At an envelope peak the two agree to within about one percent; between events they do not agree at all. Neither is a median filter, so neither is bit-for-bit what AASPI will hand you. The sections here are scaled to their own maximum, since the raw value has no interpretable units, and the curve in step 5 is drawn on a fixed axis so that changing a slider changes the curve rather than the scale.

The volume, for step 6. The first five steps use one line. Step 6 needs a map, so the model is extended to 48 crosslines, and extended so that crossline 12 is exactly the line the other steps show: the cross-dip is zero there, the gas lobe still covers it completely, and the channel that appears in module 03's map never comes closer than six crosslines. The harness rebuilds the reference crossline out of the volume and compares it against the section sample by sample. Two features exist only in map view: the gas fill pinches out against a wavy edge in the crossline direction, and there is a soft meandering channel 78 ms below the sand. The horizon is the top of the sand taken from the model rather than picked, so it carries no picking error. Only the times a horizon window can reach are computed, and the band-limited noise field is reused across crosslines with an offset rather than generated independently for each.

Simplifications, and where this departs from AASPI's instantaneous_attributes. The program computes on a 3D volume and offers a median filter over a user-defined window to stabilize sweetness and phase unwrapping; there is no median filter here. Unwrapped phase, which the program produces by the method of Vesnaver (2017) — integrating dφ/dt rather than repairing the jumps — is implemented in the shared library and used to state the definition, but is not displayed in this module because it has no useful color scale in a beginner's first pass. The wavelet attribute assignment here uses the nearest envelope maximum rather than an explicit search between adjacent minima; on these traces the two agree, but on a very noisy trace they need not. The synthetic is acoustic, normal-incidence, and one-dimensional: no multiples, no transmission losses, no offset, no anisotropy, and a wavelet that does not change shape with depth except in the gas window.
