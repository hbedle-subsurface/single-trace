Module 05  ·  Single-trace attributes

Amplitude volume transform: three things you already know

There is nothing new in this module. Take the envelope from module 01. Take its running RMS from module 03. Then take a Hilbert transform, from module 01 again. That is the recipe. What makes it worth a module is what the combination does: it turns a display of interfaces into a display of packages, and it loses about a third of the frequency doing it.

1Envelope, RMS, Hilbert

Four panels, three of which you have seen before

The panels below are the recipe in order, left to right.

  • The trace. Reflections from interfaces, positive and negative.
  • The envelope, from module 01: how strong the reflection is at each instant, with polarity and phase thrown away. Never negative.
  • The RMS of that envelope, from module 03: a running window slid down it. Smoother again, and still never negative.
  • The Hilbert transform of that, which makes it bipolar and gives it something to display. This is the amplitude volume transform.

The technique came out of Brazil in the late 1990s, where Bulhões called it tecnica volume de amplitudes, and the idea behind it was the elementary seismic layer: stop trying to interpret the reflections and interpret the packages between them instead.

That idea is the reason to keep the rock in mind while reading this module. Under the line are layers: a thinning wedge, a gas-charged sand with its brine-filled equivalent along strike, thin interbeds, and, in the volume step 6 uses, a channel 78 ms below the top of the sand. Each boundary between two layers of different acoustic impedance returns a reflection coefficient, and the trace is those coefficients convolved with the wavelet. Module 02 draws that chain panel by panel if the step from a rock boundary to a wiggle is not yet automatic. What AVT is trying to recover is the layer between the boundaries — the channel fill rather than its top and base — which is the same thing a geologist wants and the trace does not offer.

2Why take a Hilbert transform of it

And where the events end up

Stop at step three and you have an all-positive attribute. That is a genuinely awkward thing to look at. Every value is on one side of zero, so a section of it is all one color, there are no zero crossings to autotrack, and picking a boundary means picking the middle of a gradient by eye. Envelope displays have this problem too, which is why they are usually shown on a one-sided color bar and rarely picked directly.

The Hilbert transform fixes the display problem in one step. Applied to a positive hump, it returns a wiggle that is zero at the center of the hump and largest at its edges, one side positive and the other negative. So an AVT section has zero crossings, has polarity, and can be autotracked — and the events on it mark the edges of energy packages rather than the packages themselves.

The AVT's own peak is — further out, on the edge of the package. You have met this rotation before: in module 02 integration turned the trace by 90 degrees and moved the events off the interfaces and onto the layers. Same rotation, same kind of favour.

3It comes out lower frequency

A real cost, not a side effect

The AASPI documentation notes in passing that AVT results are lower frequency than the data they came from. It is worth understanding why, because it puts a limit on what the attribute can resolve.

Taking an envelope is demodulation. The trace is a slow shape multiplied by a fast oscillation, and the envelope keeps the slow shape and discards the oscillation. What is left has a bandwidth similar to the original but centered near zero rather than near the dominant frequency. Then the running RMS smooths it further. By the time you reach the Hilbert transform, the high frequencies are long gone.

Lower frequency means poorer vertical resolution, and there is no way to get it back. Worse, the loss is not a fixed fraction: taking the wavelet from 12 Hz to 40 Hz moves the seismic mean from 10.9 to 34.5 Hz and the AVT mean only from 7.2 to 10.5 Hz. Better data buys you far less on this attribute than it buys you on the amplitude display. If two beds are separated well enough to be distinguished on the amplitude display but not on the AVT, that is the transform talking. Use the amplitude data for anything about thickness and timing, and the AVT for the shape and continuity of packages, which is what it was designed for.

4The window, again

Same lesson as module 03, one step further along

The running RMS in the middle of the recipe has a window length, and it does here exactly what it did in module 03: it decides what counts as a package.

A short window keeps the individual events separate, and the AVT ends up looking like a slightly smoothed, phase-rotated version of the data. A long window merges neighboring events into single blocks, which is what the technique was built for, at the cost of not being able to tell you what is inside them. The wedge on the left of this line is a good place to watch the two behaviors trade off, because it has two events that get closer together across the section.

Two windows are now stacked in this attribute: the wavelet, which sets what the seismic could resolve in the first place, and the RMS window, which sets what the transform can resolve after that. The second can only ever be worse than the first. If you find yourself lengthening the window to make the display look cleaner, be clear with yourself that you are choosing to see less.

5Beside relative acoustic impedance

Two ways to make a layer display, and one big difference

Module 02 produced a layer-like display by integrating the trace. This module produces one by taking an envelope and Hilbert transforming it. They look similar on screen and they are not the same thing at all.

  • Relative acoustic impedance keeps polarity. A hard bed and a soft bed come out with opposite signs, because integration is a linear operation on a signed trace. That is why it can be read as a rock property.
  • AVT throws polarity away in its first step. The envelope is a magnitude, so a hard bed and a soft bed of the same strength are identical from that point on. AVT tells you where the energy is, not what kind.

The panels show both, computed from the same trace. Look at the sand: on the impedance display it is a low, because gas-charged sand is soft. On the AVT it is a strong event, because it is bright. Both are correct answers to different questions, and reading the second as though it were the first is a mistake that a display alone will never reveal.

The AVT section is drawn in gray rather than on a color bar, which is how it is normally shown. That is not only convention. Step 2 went to the trouble of making this attribute bipolar precisely so it could be looked at the way seismic is looked at, and a color bar undoes that by inviting you to read the values as magnitudes. They are not magnitudes. They are a wiggle.

6What it was built for

A slice, not a section

This is the display the technique exists for. AVT came out of work on seismic geomorphology — looking at depositional systems in plan view — and a section is not where that happens. You take a slice parallel to a horizon and look down on it.

The line you have been working on is one crossline out of a small volume, and there is a channel in it, 78 ms below the top of the sand and 30 ms thick. Below are two slices at the same level: the amplitude on the left, the AVT on the right. Move the slider to walk the slice down through the interval, and watch two quite different things happen.

The amplitude slice flickers. Its contrast between the channel and the rock beside it is ×5.2 at 78 ms, ×0.4 twelve milliseconds higher and ×1.4 twelve milliseconds lower, and ×7.7 at 108 ms. Move the slice by half a wavelet and the answer changes by a factor of twelve, because you are cutting through a wavelet and the level decides which lobe you land on.

Below that it wanders between ×0.3 and ×0.4, which is the attribute reading a level where there is nothing much to read. That stability is the attribute's real contribution, and the readouts below measure it: at the current level, moving the slice 12 ms either way changes the amplitude answer by — and the AVT answer by —.

There is a trap in it, though, and it is the one step 2 warned about. The AVT slice images the channel best at about 42 ms — roughly 36 ms above the channel — and reads its weakest right in the middle of it. AVT events sit on the edges of energy packages, not on the packages. Slice both attributes at the same level and you will conclude the channel is not there on the AVT.

Worth watching the RMS window in the header too: lengthen it and the channel gets fatter on the AVT slice without getting any wider in the ground.

?What it is for

A display transform with a specific job

AVT was built by interpreters for interpreting, and the job it was built for is a real one: seeing depositional packages as bodies rather than as pairs of reflections you have to mentally join up.

What it is used for

  • Seismic geomorphology. Channels, fans, bars and mounds read more clearly as shapes when the display is showing packages of energy rather than individual interfaces.
  • Stratigraphic surfaces. Onlap and downlap terminations are often easier to follow, because the events are the edges of packages and the packages are what terminates.
  • Continuity through noisy or dim data. The chain of smoothing operations is fairly forgiving, so a package can stay visible where individual reflections have become hard to follow.
  • Horizon tracking where the amplitude data has variable polarity or character along the horizon, since AVT is indifferent to both.

The four ways it will mislead you

It has no polarity, at all. The first step throws it away and nothing later recovers it. A hard streak and a soft sand of equal brightness are the same event on an AVT display. If your interpretation depends on knowing which, do not use this attribute for it.

The events are not the interfaces. They mark the edges of energy packages, a quarter cycle away from the RMS envelope peaks. Picking an AVT event and calling it a formation top will put the pick in the wrong place, consistently and by a predictable amount.

The resolution is worse than the data's. Measurably so: at the default settings the AVT's mean frequency is two and a half times lower than the seismic's, and the gap widens as the data improves — step 3 has the numbers. Anything you cannot separate on it may well be separable on the amplitude display.

It inherits every amplitude decision, twice. Both the envelope and the RMS scale directly with the input amplitudes, so gain applied upstream flows straight through — and module 04 showed what that does. An AVT volume made from AGC'd data is a picture of the AGC window.

The honest summary: AVT does not measure anything the trace did not already contain. It rearranges what is there to suit a particular kind of looking, and it does that well. Trouble starts when a rearrangement gets treated as a measurement.

Exercises

  1. Watch a positive quantity become bipolar. In step 2, read what fraction of the AVT is below zero, and compare it against its own input.
    HintThe input to the last step, the RMS envelope, is positive at every one of the 441 samples in the window — it is a square root, so it cannot be anything else. The output is below zero at 68% of them. No information was created and none was destroyed: a Hilbert transform is a phase rotation, and it keeps every frequency at the same strength it had. What it does is move the whole thing off one side of zero, so the result has zero crossings to track and two colors to display. If that sounds like a cosmetic operation, it is, and it is the reason anybody uses the attribute.
  2. Find the frequency you lost. In step 3, read both peak frequencies, then change the dominant frequency in the header from 12 Hz to 45 Hz and read them again.
    HintAt 25 Hz the seismic averages 22.5 Hz and the AVT averages 8.9 Hz, a factor of 2.5. Now sweep the slider. At 12 Hz the two read 10.9 and 7.2 Hz. At 40 Hz they read 34.5 and 10.5 Hz. The seismic number follows the wavelet, tripling across that range. The AVT number moves from 7.2 to 10.5 and no further. Its frequency is set by the envelope's own bandwidth and by the RMS window, not by the data. The practical consequence is uncomfortable: buying better bandwidth improves your amplitude display a great deal and your AVT display hardly at all.
  3. Make the window do the damage. In step 4, take the RMS window from ±4 ms to ±80 ms and watch how many separate events survive on the trace.
    HintThe trace itself carries 12 events above the threshold. Its AVT carries 7 at ±4 ms, 5 at ±24, 2 at ±60 and 1 at ±80. By the longest setting the entire second half of the trace has become a single feature. Nothing merged in the earth. Two windows are now stacked in this attribute — the wavelet, which decides what the seismic could resolve at all, and the RMS window, which decides what survives the transform — and the second can only ever make things blunter than the first.
  4. Prove the polarity is gone. In step 5, read the two polarity tests and think about what each one is telling you.
    HintNegate the trace and recompute. Relative acoustic impedance correlates at −1.000 against its original: it flipped completely, which is what any linear operation on a signed trace must do. AVT correlates at +1.000: it did not change by one sample. The envelope of a trace and the envelope of its negative are the same function, so from the first step onward AVT has no way of knowing which way up the data was. That is fine if you are mapping where the energy is. It is fatal if you are trying to tell a hard streak from a soft sand, and no amount of looking at the display will warn you, because the display looks the same either way.
  5. Put the pick in the wrong place. In step 2, compare the time of the RMS envelope peak against the nearest AVT zero crossing and the AVT peak.
    HintThe RMS envelope peaks at 488 ms. The AVT crosses zero at 490 ms, two milliseconds away, which within one sample is the same place. Its own peak is 60 ms further on, at 548 ms. So a pick made on the bright part of an AVT display sits about 60 ms from the center of the package that produced it, and a pick made on the zero crossing sits on it. That is the opposite of the habit most people bring from amplitude displays, where the bright part is the thing. On this attribute the bright parts are the edges.
  6. Move a slice and watch an answer change. In step 6, set the slice to 78 ms, where the channel is, and read how much each attribute's contrast moves when the slice moves 12 ms either way. Then do it again at 42 ms and at 108 ms.
    HintAt 78 ms the amplitude reads ×5.23 on the channel, ×0.43 twelve milliseconds above it and ×1.42 twelve below. Highest over lowest across those three levels is a factor of 12.2, which is what the swing readout reports, and it is the drop off the center value that does most of it. The AVT over the same 24 ms swings by 3.4. At 108 ms it is 6.0 against 1.5, and at 42 ms it is 2.4 against 1.3. The amplitude slice is unstable because it is cutting through a wavelet and the level decides which lobe it lands on; the AVT slice is steadier because the envelope threw the wavelet away in step 1. That is the honest case for slicing this attribute rather than the amplitude: not that it sees more, but that it does not change its mind when your horizon pick moves by a few milliseconds — which, on real data, it will.
  7. One strong AVT event, and what it might be. An AVT slice shows a strong, coherent event across part of the survey. AVT is built from the envelope, so list what could produce it, and say which of those a normal amplitude display would separate for you.
    HintA strong AVT response means a package with more energy in it than its surroundings. That could be a genuine impedance contrast, hard or soft — step 5 showed AVT cannot tell you which, because the envelope threw polarity away in the first step. It could be constructive interference between two closely spaced reflectors, which raises energy without either contrast changing. It could be a thicker package rather than a stronger one, since the RMS in the middle of the recipe rewards length as well as strength. Or it could be upstream gain, which raises the envelope and therefore the AVT everywhere it was applied. The amplitude display separates the first of these from the others immediately, because it keeps polarity: a hard bed is a peak and a soft bed is a trough, and on AVT they are identical. That is the practical rule for this attribute. Use it to find bodies and to follow their shape; go back to the amplitude data, or to module 02's impedance display, to find out what the body is.

The main points

  • Three steps, all of them familiar. Envelope, running RMS of the envelope, Hilbert transform. Nothing in AVT is new; the combination is the idea.
  • The Hilbert transform at the end is for display. It turns an all-positive quantity into a bipolar one with zero crossings, adding no information whatsoever.
  • The events mark package edges, a quarter cycle from the RMS envelope peaks, not the interfaces that produced them.
  • The result is lower frequency than the data — two and a half times lower at the default settings on this line — so its vertical resolution is worse and cannot be recovered. The ratio is not fixed either: it is x1.5 at a 12 Hz wavelet and x3.3 at 40 Hz, because the AVT barely moves when the data gets better.
  • Polarity is gone from the first step onward. Hard and soft look identical.
  • It is a slice attribute. AVT was built for looking at depositional systems in plan view, and its advantage over an amplitude slice is stability rather than sensitivity: move the slice 12 ms either way at the channel and the amplitude answer swings by a factor of twelve where the AVT answer swings by three.
  • Two window lengths now govern the answer: the wavelet, and the RMS window you chose. The second can only make things blunter than the first.
Method, sources, and what has been simplified

The data is the same synthetic line as modules 01, 03 and 04: 96 traces of 500 samples at 2 ms, reflection coefficients convolved with a Ricker wavelet over a low-relief anticline, with a wedge, a gas sand and its brine equivalent, interbeds and a dimming reflector. The identical model code runs in all four modules and the harness compares their output sample by sample, so the line cannot drift between them.

The attribute follows AASPI's avt exactly as its documentation gives it. The analytic trace is formed, the envelope taken as its magnitude, the RMS of the envelope computed in a running window of 2K+1 samples, and the inverse Hilbert transform applied to the result. The inverse Hilbert transform is the negative of the forward one, which is what makes the output bipolar; if your implementation appears to differ from another package by a sign, that is where to look first.

The spectra in step 3 are amplitude spectra computed by discrete Fourier transform over the drawn window, each normalized to its own peak so the shapes can be compared. Peak frequency is read as the largest bin; bandwidth is the width where the spectrum falls to half its peak power. Both quantities are read from the running page, not assumed.

The polarity test in step 5 negates the whole trace, recomputes both transforms, and correlates each against its original. A linear transform returns exactly −1; a magnitude-based one returns +1. Relative acoustic impedance is computed here with the same integrate routine module 02 uses, with a 5 Hz filter corner, so the two modules are running the same code on different models.

The volume, for step 6. The first five steps use one line. Step 6 needs a slice, so the model is extended to 48 crosslines using the same block modules 01 and 03 use, and extended so that crossline 12 is exactly the line the other steps show. The harness rebuilds that crossline out of the volume and compares it against the section sample by sample. The channel is a soft meandering fill about 30 ms thick sitting 78 ms below the top of the sand, and the gas fill of the sand pinches out against a wavy edge in the crossline direction; neither is visible on any section the earlier steps draw. The slices are single samples rather than window averages, because that is how a stratal slice is made and because averaging a bipolar attribute over an interval would cancel it against itself. The horizon is taken from the model rather than picked, so it carries no picking error — which is worth remembering when reading the stability numbers, since on real data the pick is exactly what moves.

The slice is interpolated between the two nearest samples rather than rounded to one. A horizon moves smoothly across a survey and a rounded sample index does not, so rounding puts a one-sample hop into the extraction wherever the horizon crosses a sample boundary. On a single-sample slice through a wavelet that is a large change in value, and it draws as stripes running along the inline direction — a map that looks like acquisition footprint and is nothing of the kind.

Simplifications. One dimension for the first five steps, normal incidence, acoustic, no multiples and no transmission losses. AASPI runs on a 3D volume. There is no spectral balancing step here; on real data a wavelet closer to a spike changes the envelope and therefore everything downstream of it. The original technique is described in two Brazilian conference papers, both in Portuguese, and the account here follows the AASPI documentation's summary of them rather than the originals.
