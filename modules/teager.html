Module 06  ·  Single-trace attributes

Teager-Kaiser energy: the one that counts frequency too

Every attribute so far that measures strength — envelope, RMS, AVT — measures amplitude and nothing else. A physicist would object. The energy in an oscillation depends on how fast it is oscillating as well as how far, and a quiet, high-frequency event can carry as much energy as a loud, slow one. Teager-Kaiser energy is that objection turned into an attribute, and it comes with three complications that are worth more than the attribute itself.

1Energy needs two numbers, not one

A mass on a spring, and a seismic wave

Hang a mass on a spring and set it going. Its total energy is E = ½ m ω² A²: it depends on how far it swings, and just as much on how fast. Double the amplitude and you quadruple the energy. Double the frequency and you quadruple it again.

Sheriff and Geldart give the energy density of a seismic wave as E = ½ ρ ω² A². Apart from the mass becoming a density, it is the same expression. So a seismic trace already contains an energy, and it is not the envelope: the envelope is A, and energy goes as A²ω².

Kaiser's contribution in 1990 was to notice that this quantity can be recovered from three consecutive samples, with no transform and no window:

ψ(n) = x(n)² − x(n+1)·x(n−1)

The panels below put the envelope and the Teager-Kaiser energy side by side on the same trace. They do not agree, and where they disagree the trace is changing frequency. Watch the wedge in particular, where interference shifts the local frequency around.

A word on where the line comes from, since the whole module is read against it. Under it is a set of layers: a thinning wedge, a gas-charged sand with the same sand brine-filled along strike, a package of thin interbeds, and a reflector that dims across the section. Each boundary between two layers of different acoustic impedance returns a reflection coefficient, those coefficients are convolved with the wavelet, and the result is the trace the attribute is computed from. Module 02 draws that chain out panel by panel — impedance, then reflectivity, then trace — and it is worth looking at once if the connection between a rock boundary and a wiggle is not yet automatic. Frequency is the part of the chain this module cares about: gas attenuates the high frequencies passing through it, and thin beds raise the local frequency by interference, so both show up in an attribute that counts frequency as well as amplitude.

2Two formulas, and why AASPI uses the second

Three samples against two derivatives

The three-sample formula is exact for a sampled cosine, but what it returns is A²sin²(ωΔt), not A²ω². Those agree while ωΔt is small, and drift apart as it is not. For a pure tone at a 2 ms sample interval the error is about 1% at 12 Hz, 5% at 30 Hz and 18% at 60 Hz — always low, always worse with frequency.

Kaiser's 1993 continuous form avoids that by using derivatives instead of neighboring samples:

ψ(t) = (dx/dt)² − x·(d²x/dt²)

which returns A²ω² exactly, at any frequency. It has its own problem, though: differentiating amplifies high frequencies, and doing it twice amplifies them twice. On noisy data that is a serious objection. AASPI's answer, following Matos, is to use Holoborodko's smooth noise-robust differentiators — eleven-point and nine-point filters designed to differentiate while suppressing the high end.

Which buys the noise rejection at a price nobody advertises. Those filters are smoothed, so they roll off with frequency too: on the same pure tone, measured against the exact answer, they read 98% at 12 Hz, 88% at 30 Hz and 66% at 60 Hz. Both practical formulas under-read, and both get worse with frequency.

The readouts below are lower than those two lists, and the reason is worth knowing. Both lists describe a single frequency. A Ricker wavelet carries energy well above the number on the frequency slider, and both formulas do their worst work up there, so the shortfall measured on a real wavelet is larger than the pure-tone table predicts at the same nominal frequency.

3The ripple, and the complex fix

Why the energy of a real trace pulses

Look closely at the Teager-Kaiser energy of the raw trace and you will see it oscillating within each event, at twice the frequency of the event itself. That is not noise. A real cosine's energy genuinely does pulse: the mass on the spring trades kinetic energy for potential energy twice per cycle, and a formula built on the instantaneous state sees that exchange.

For interpretation that ripple is a nuisance, and there is a clean way out. Hamila and colleagues showed that the Teager-Kaiser energy of a complex signal is the sum of the energies of its real and imaginary parts. Take the analytic trace from module 01 — the trace and its Hilbert transform — compute the energy of both, and add. The two ripples are a quarter cycle out of step and cancel, leaving a smooth result.

It is the same trick that made the envelope smooth in module 01, applied to a different quantity. The panels below show both, and the readouts measure how much ripple survives in each. At the default 25 Hz the improvement is real but modest. Take the dominant frequency up to 45 Hz, where each cycle covers fewer samples, and it becomes obvious: the real-trace energy breaks into three separate humps inside a single event and the analytic version stays as one.

Ripple here is measured against each curve's own smoothed version, over the strongest event on the trace, so that the rise and fall of the event itself does not get counted as pulsation. At 25 Hz the real-trace energy carries 47% ripple and the analytic version 28%; at 45 Hz it is 81% against 33%. The cancellation is exact only for a pure tone, and a seismic event is not one, so what you get is a large reduction rather than a clean removal. If you are ever handed a Teager-Kaiser volume that looks strangely striped at twice the dominant frequency, this is why, and it means the complex form was not used.

4Cross terms: energies do not add

The reason this attribute needs care on real data

Here is the awkward property. Put two frequencies into the operator at once and the answer is not the sum of what each would have given on its own. There are extra terms that depend on both, and they can be larger than either contribution.

The panel below does exactly that experiment on this trace's own frequencies. The trace is split at 26 Hz into a low band and a high band. The first curve is the energy of the low band alone, the second of the high band alone, the third of the two together, and the fourth is the difference between the third and the sum of the first two — the cross term, which should be nothing and is not.

Across this trace the cross term has an RMS of — of the two parts taken together, and at its worst single sample it reaches twelve times their mean. A seismic trace is never one frequency, so this is the normal case rather than a special one. Kaiser identified the problem in 1993, and the standard answer is to split the trace into narrow frequency bands first, compute the energy in each, and put them back together — which is what Matos and colleagues did with a wavelet transform, and what AASPI's implementation works around by using the continuous operator with smooth derivatives.

This does not make the attribute broken. It does mean that Teager-Kaiser energy of a broadband trace is a different quantity from the sum of the energies of its parts, so it cannot be interpreted band by band, and two volumes computed with different preconditioning are not comparable. If somebody hands you a TK volume, the question to ask is what was done to the data first.

5Making it displayable

The same fix module 05 used, for the same reason

The energy sits on one side of zero nearly everywhere, so a Teager-Kaiser volume has the display problem module 05 ran into: one color, no zero crossings to speak of, nothing to autotrack. The few negative samples step 1 mentioned are far too small to give the display anything to work with. The answer here is the same one, and it comes from the same Brazilian work: bandpass it, then Hilbert transform it. Matos calls the result the Teager-Kaiser variation, TKV.

The bandpass is doing real work. Energy is squared amplitude, so it has a large constant component and a lot of very low frequency content, and Hilbert transforming that unfiltered gives a result dominated by the slow trend. The filter corner slider in the header controls it: take it to zero to switch the filter off and watch the attribute go soft.

?What it adds

The one strength attribute that is not just amplitude

Everything else in this set that measures how much is happening measures amplitude. This one measures amplitude and frequency together, which is either the reason to use it or the reason to be careful with it, depending on what you are trying to see.

What it is used for

  • Thin beds and tuning. A thin bed raises the local frequency as well as changing the amplitude, and an attribute sensitive to both responds more strongly than one sensitive to amplitude alone.
  • Stratigraphic edges. The variational form is used the way AVT is, as a display that shows packages rather than interfaces, with the frequency term adding contrast at bed boundaries.
  • Discontinuity detection. The energy responds sharply where waveform character changes, so the variational form is sometimes used as an input to coherence rather than the raw amplitude.
  • Attenuation and gas. A low-frequency shadow lowers the energy twice over, through ω² and through amplitude, so anomalies stand out more than on an envelope.

The four ways it will mislead you

Amplitude and frequency arrive mixed together. A bright anomaly and a high-frequency one look the same, and the attribute cannot separate them for you. If the question is which of the two is happening, you need the envelope and a frequency attribute side by side, not their product.

Energies do not add. On a broadband trace the cross terms are real and can exceed the terms they sit between. The number at a sample is not decomposable into contributions from the frequencies present.

Both practical formulas under-read, and unevenly. The three-sample form falls off as ωΔt grows; the noise-robust derivative filters fall off too. Two implementations of the same attribute can differ by tens of percent at the high end and agree at the low end, which is a difficult kind of disagreement to notice.

It squares the amplitude. Everything module 04 said about inherited gain applies here twice over: a factor of two in the input is a factor of four in the output. An AGC'd volume makes this attribute meaningless faster than it does the envelope.

Worth carrying: this is the only attribute in the set that measures something the envelope genuinely cannot. It is also the one with the most ways to be computed slightly differently, and the one where two vendors' answers are least likely to match.

Exercises

  1. Find where energy and envelope disagree. In step 1, read the correlation between them, then change the dominant frequency and read it again.
    HintAt the default settings the envelope and the Teager-Kaiser energy correlate at 0.831 on this trace. Not 1.000, which is the point: if it were, the attribute would be an expensive envelope. What keeps it away from 1 is the frequency term. Change the dominant frequency and the correlation moves a little — 0.857 at 12 Hz, 0.865 at 45 Hz — but it never reaches 1, because the two quantities are genuinely different. Where they part company is where the local frequency is changing: interference in the wedge, and the low-frequency shadow under the gas sand.
  2. Catch the formulas under-reading. In step 2, compare the three-sample and Holoborodko results against the exact one, then raise the dominant frequency to 45 Hz.
    HintMeasured across the strongest event on the trace, at 25 Hz the Holoborodko filters return 84% of the exact energy and the three-sample formula returns 94%. Raise the wavelet to 45 Hz and they fall to 63% and 83%. Drop it to 12 Hz and they rise to 98% and 99%. Both approximations are good at low frequency and both under-read at high frequency, for different reasons: the three-sample form is a small-angle approximation, and the Holoborodko filters are deliberately smoothed to reject noise. These are further below the exact answer than the pure-tone lists in step 2, and deliberately so: those describe one frequency, and a wavelet with a 25 Hz peak carries plenty above 25 Hz, where both formulas are at their worst. Notice which is closer here. The plain three-sample formula is more accurate on clean data; the filters earn their place only when there is noise to reject, which is a trade the documentation does not spell out.
  3. Kill the ripple. In step 3, read the ripple in the real-trace energy and in the analytic-trace version.
    HintAt 25 Hz the real-trace energy carries 47% ripple against its own smooth level, and the analytic version carries 28%. Both show one hump per event, so at this frequency the effect is real but easy to miss. Now take the wavelet to 45 Hz: the real version goes to 81% ripple and breaks into three separate humps inside a single event, while the analytic version stays at 33% and one hump. The ripple is at twice the signal frequency, so the faster the data the worse it looks, and the more obviously the complex form is worth the extra Hilbert transform.
  4. Measure a cross term. In step 4, compare the two components together against the sum of them separately.
    HintSplit into a band below 26 Hz and a band above it, the energies of the two parts do not add up to the energy of the whole. The difference has an RMS of 111% of the two parts combined, and at its worst single sample it reaches twelve times their mean. Turn the dominant frequency down to 12 Hz, so that almost everything sits in the low band and the high band is nearly empty, and the cross term falls to 19% — the problem is smallest when there is effectively only one frequency present, which is exactly what the theory says. A real seismic trace is never that. The consequence is that a Teager-Kaiser value cannot be decomposed into contributions from the frequencies in the data, and two volumes computed after different filtering are not comparable.
  5. Switch the TKV filter off. In step 5, take the filter corner from 6 Hz down to 0 and watch what happens to the attribute.
    HintWith the corner at 6 Hz the TKV correlates with the AVT at 0.302 and spends 51% of its samples below zero. Take the corner to 0, switching the filter off, and the correlation rises to 0.634 while 76% of the samples go below zero and the whole curve sags. That is the slow trend in the energy taking over: energy is a squared quantity, so it has a large constant part and a lot of very low frequency content, and a Hilbert transform of that returns something dominated by the trend rather than by the events. Push the corner the other way, to 14 Hz, and the correlation goes to −0.084 — you have filtered away so much that the attribute no longer resembles anything. The corner is a real choice with a narrow sensible range.
  6. One high-energy zone, and why the number cannot tell you which. A Teager-Kaiser volume shows a zone of high energy. Energy goes as amplitude squared times frequency squared, so name at least two situations that would raise it, and say what you would compute to separate them.
    HintBoth ingredients raise it independently, so a bright low-frequency event and a quiet high-frequency one can return the same energy. A tuned thin bed raises both at once, which is why the attribute responds to thin beds more strongly than the envelope does. A strong impedance contrast raises amplitude alone. A stack of thin interbeds raises the local frequency alone. And because energy squares the amplitude, any gain applied upstream is squared too — a factor of two in becomes a factor of four out. The attribute mixes amplitude and frequency and cannot unmix them for you, so the separation has to be done outside it: compute the envelope from module 01 and a wavelet frequency from step 4 of the same module, and read both at the anomaly. High envelope with normal frequency is a contrast effect. Normal envelope with high frequency is thin bedding. Both raised together is tuning. This is the general rule for any attribute that multiplies two quantities: the product is a screening tool, and the interpretation is in the factors.

The main points

  • Energy goes as amplitude squared times frequency squared. The envelope measures only the amplitude. This is the one attribute in the set that measures both.
  • There are two formulas and neither is exact in practice. The three-sample form returns A²sin²(ωΔt); the smooth derivative filters roll off with frequency. Both under-read the high end.
  • The energy of a real trace ripples at twice the signal frequency. Computing it on the analytic trace instead cancels the ripple, exactly as the envelope does in module 01.
  • Cross terms are real and large. On a trace with more than one frequency present, the energy is not the sum of the energies of the parts.
  • The energy is one-sided nearly everywhere, so the displayable version is bandpassed and Hilbert transformed. That is the same construction as AVT, applied to a different quantity. It is not strictly positive: the cross terms drive it slightly below zero on a broadband trace, which is a fact about the operator rather than a problem with the display.
  • It squares the input amplitude, so it inherits every upstream gain decision twice over.
Method, sources, and what has been simplified

The data is the same synthetic line as modules 01, 03, 04 and 05: 96 traces of 500 samples at 2 ms, over a low-relief anticline, with a wedge, a gas sand and its brine equivalent, interbeds and a dimming reflector. The identical model code runs in all five and the harness compares their output sample by sample.

The three formulas. The discrete form is Kaiser (1990), ψ(n) = x(n)² − x(n+1)x(n−1). The continuous form is Kaiser (1993), ψ(t) = (dx/dt)² − x d²x/dt², computed two ways here: with the Holoborodko noise-robust differentiators AASPI specifies, and with exact spectral derivatives, which are not practical on noisy data but serve as the reference the other two are measured against. The verification harness confirms that the continuous form returns A²ω² exactly for a pure tone and that the discrete form returns A²sin²(ωΔt) exactly, which is what makes the comparison in step 2 a measurement rather than an assertion.

The Holoborodko filters, and a correction. The AASPI documentation prints an eleven-point first-derivative filter and a nine-point second-derivative filter. The first is correct: its coefficients sum to zero and it is exact on a straight line. The second is printed with the signs of the two coefficients either side of the center reversed, so that as printed they sum to 48 rather than 0 — a filter that would report a second derivative of 0.25 for a constant. The set used here keeps the same numbers with those two signs corrected, which satisfies both requirements: the coefficients sum to zero, and the filter returns exactly 2 for t². This is treated as a typesetting error in the document rather than a different filter.

The cross-term experiment in step 4 splits the actual trace into a low band and a high band with the shared Ormsby filter, computes the energy of each separately and of their sum, and reports the difference. That is a real decomposition of real data rather than a pair of synthetic tones, so the numbers describe this trace and would differ on another.

Simplifications, and where this departs from AASPI. One dimension, normal incidence, acoustic. AASPI computes on 3D volumes and, following Matos (2018), also offers energy of the complex trace, which is implemented here as step 3. The subband decomposition that Matos and colleagues used to avoid cross terms — a continuous wavelet transform, with the energy computed per voice — is described in step 4 but not implemented; doing it properly needs the spectral decomposition machinery, which belongs to a different set of modules. The TKV bandpass here is a four-point Ormsby with its low corner on the slider and its high corner fixed well above the data.
