Module 04  ·  Single-trace attributes

AGC: making everything equally bright

Take the RMS amplitude you computed in module 03 and divide the trace by it. That is the whole operation. Deep reflectors that were invisible come up to full strength, the section becomes easy to look at from top to bottom, and every amplitude interpretation you were about to do becomes impossible. This module is about the trade, and about why AGC done quietly to your data upstream is the most common way an amplitude study goes wrong.

1Divide the trace by its own loudness

Two steps, and the first one is module 03

AGC is defined in two lines. Compute the RMS amplitude in a running window, exactly as in module 03. Then divide every sample by the RMS at that sample:

dAGC(t) = d(t) / σ(t)

That is all of it. Where the trace is loud, σ is large and the division shrinks it. Where the trace is quiet, σ is small and the division blows it up. The result is a trace whose loudness is roughly constant all the way down, because it has been defined to be.

The middle panel is the divisor. Read it as the gain curve being applied, upside down: wherever it is high the data is being turned down, and wherever it is low the data is being turned up. At the cursor the trace is being multiplied by —.

Notice that the gain is different at every sample and on every trace. Nothing coordinates them. Two neighbouring traces with different amounts of energy in their windows get different gains, so after AGC the amplitude relationship between traces has been rewritten too — not just the relationship down a trace. The AASPI documentation is blunt about this: trace-to-trace AGC is a poor choice before any true amplitude analysis.

2What it fixes

The reason it is on by default nearly everywhere

Seismic amplitudes fall off with depth, badly. Energy spreads out as the wavefront grows, and the earth absorbs the higher frequencies as it goes. By a second or two, reflections from real, interpretable geology can be a hundredth the size of the shallow ones. Displayed honestly, the bottom half of the section is a gray smear.

The decay slider in the header controls how severe that is here. At the default setting the shallow marker on this trace is — times the size of the deep one. After AGC the same two events are — apart. That is the entire case for the process, and it is a strong one: you cannot interpret what you cannot see.

This is also why AGC is nearly always already on. Every display package applies some form of gain by default, often without saying so in any obvious place. If you have never turned it off, you have probably never seen your data.

3What it costs

The bright spot was the point, and it is gone

This line has a gas sand on the right and the same sand, brine-filled, on the left. On the amplitude display that contrast is the most important thing in the picture: it is what a direct hydrocarbon indicator is.

AGC does not know that. It sees a stretch of trace with more energy in it than its neighbours, and its job is to remove exactly that difference. The gas sand is — brighter than the brine sand before AGC and — after it. The anomaly has been divided away by a process running on autopilot.

Nothing on the display announces this. The AGC section looks cleaner, more balanced and more professional than the one it came from. It is the more attractive picture and the less informative one, and if you were handed it without being told, there is no way to tell from the screen that a bright spot ever existed.

4Long windows are gentler than short ones

The one setting that decides how much damage is done

The window length controls how local the gain is, and therefore how much of the real amplitude variation gets removed along with the unwanted trend.

  • A long window — several hundred milliseconds — only sees the slow fall-off with depth. It corrects that and leaves most of the local contrast alone. This is the safe setting, and the reason AASPI's default is ±500 ms.
  • A short window follows the data so closely that it removes almost everything, including the anomaly you were looking for. At the extreme it starts flattening within a single wavelet, which distorts the waveform itself.

The panel below shows the same trace at three window lengths, with the sand marked. The readouts measure two costs: how much of the gas-to-brine contrast survives, and how much the shape of an individual wavelet has been altered, measured as a correlation against the original.

The waveform numbers are correlations between the shape of the shallow marker before and after AGC. A long window leaves it essentially untouched. A short one does not: dividing by a quantity that changes across the wavelet stretches one side of it relative to the other, which shifts the apparent peak and changes the phase. If you are picking horizons on AGC'd data with a short window, your picks are being moved by the processing.

5Everything downstream inherits it

The reason this module exists at all

AGC is not usually applied by the person doing the interpretation. It is applied earlier, by someone else, for a display that was perfectly reasonable at the time. Then the volume gets passed along, and attributes get computed from it.

Here is what that does. The panel below computes the envelope from module 01 two ways: from the original trace, and from the AGC'd trace. On the original, the gas sand's envelope is — times the brine sand's. On the AGC'd version it is —. The envelope is a perfectly good attribute and it has been told a lie.

The same applies to everything else in this set that depends on amplitude: RMS, of course, since AGC is defined as division by it; sweetness, which is envelope over a root frequency; and relative acoustic impedance, whose whole output scales with the trace it integrated. Only the attributes that already threw amplitude away survive — instantaneous phase and instantaneous frequency come through unharmed, because they are ratios in which the gain cancels.

So the question to ask before computing any amplitude attribute is not what the attribute does. It is what was done to the amplitudes before you got them.

The last two readouts are correlations between the phase and the frequency computed before and after AGC, taken over the samples that have enough energy to have a meaningful frequency at all — module 01 spent a step on why that qualifier is needed. Both sit close to 1, because each of those attributes already divides the trace by its own strength, so a gain applied beforehand cancels. That is a genuinely useful property: on data whose processing history nobody can tell you, the phase-based attributes are the ones still worth trusting, which is why phase makes such a good horizon-tracking attribute on old volumes.

?When to use it and when not to

A display tool that keeps being used as a processing step

AGC is not a bad process. It is a display process being used, very often, on data that is about to be interpreted quantitatively, and that is a different thing.

Where it is the right tool

  • Looking at the data. Structural interpretation, fault picking, seeing whether the deep section has anything in it at all. If the question is where things are rather than how bright they are, AGC costs you nothing you needed.
  • Horizon tracking, with a long window. An autotracker works better when events are of comparable strength across the survey.
  • Quality control. Balanced data makes acquisition footprint, noise bursts and processing artifacts much easier to spot.
  • Correlating with something else where only shape matters — a synthetic tie, a crossline comparison, a display for a talk.

Where it will cost you

Any amplitude interpretation, full stop. Bright spots, dim spots, AVO, impedance, RMS maps, anything with the word amplitude in it. AGC is defined as removing amplitude variation, and it cannot tell your anomaly from the trend it was asked to flatten.

Anything that compares one place against another. The gain is computed independently on every trace, so amplitudes are no longer comparable between traces, between lines or between surveys.

Precise picking on a short window. A window short enough to vary across the wavelet changes the waveform, which moves the peak and the zero crossings by a small but real amount.

Anything computed from the volume afterwards. This is the one that actually happens. Nobody sets out to interpret AGC'd amplitudes; they inherit a volume, compute an attribute and never ask. Step 5 measures what that costs.

The practical habit: when a volume arrives, find out whether gain was applied, what kind and over what window, and write it down where the interpretation will be read. If nobody knows, treat every amplitude number from that volume as a relative comparison within a single trace and nothing more.

Exercises

  1. Measure what AGC recovers. In step 2, read the shallow-to-deep ratio before and after, then set the decay to 24 dB/s and read both again.
    HintAt the default 14 dB per second the shallow marker reads 0.772 and the deep one 0.097 — a factor of eight. After AGC they are 4.011 and 3.279, a factor of 1.2. Push the decay to 24 dB/s and the difference before AGC becomes nineteenfold, with the deep event down at 0.034, and after AGC it is still 1.3. That is the process doing its job, and doing it well. Now set the decay to zero. The two events still differ by a factor of 2.4, because they are genuinely different reflectors, and AGC still flattens them to 1.2. It cannot tell the difference between a gain problem and geology, because from inside a single trace there is no difference to see.
  2. Measure what it destroys. In step 3, read the gas-to-brine contrast before and after, then take the AGC window from ±300 ms down to ±10 ms.
    HintBefore AGC the gas sand is ×3.2 brighter than the brine sand at every window, since the window has nothing to do with the recorded data. After AGC: ×2.4 at ±300 ms, ×2.0 at ±200, ×1.2 at ±120, ×0.9 at ±30 and ×0.8 at ±10. Read the last two again. Below about ±60 ms the ratio drops under 1, which means the gas sand has become dimmer than the brine sand. The anomaly has not merely been removed, it has been turned upside down, because a short window sitting inside the bright event divides it by its own brightness while the quiet brine sand gets multiplied up. A short-window AGC display can show you a dim spot where the gas is.
  3. Find the window that damages the waveform. In step 4, watch the waveform correlation as you shorten the comparison window from ±300 to ±10 ms.
    HintAt ±300, ±120 and ±60 ms the correlation is 1.0000 to four decimals: the waveform is untouched, and only its size has changed. At ±30 it is 0.9999. At ±20 it is 0.9937, and at ±10 it is 0.9773. The damage starts when the window gets short enough for the divisor to change appreciably across the wavelet itself, which for a 25 Hz wavelet means somewhere around 20 ms. Below that, one side of the wavelet is being divided by a different number than the other, which shifts the peak and moves your pick. The rule that falls out of this is the same one the AASPI documentation gives: keep the window long compared with the wavelet, and much longer than that if you care about amplitudes.
  4. Ruin an envelope map. In step 5, compare the envelope contrast computed before and after AGC, at a long window and a short one.
    HintThe envelope contrast between the two sands is ×3.2 on the original data. Computed from the AGC'd version it is ×2.4 at a ±300 ms window, ×1.2 at ±120, ×0.8 at ±30 and ×1.0 at ±10. Nothing was done to the envelope calculation. It is the same code from module 01, run on data that had already been through a process nobody told it about, and it now reports somewhere between a much weaker anomaly and no anomaly at all. This is the whole reason this module sits where it does in the set: the attribute you compute is only as trustworthy as the amplitudes you were given.
  5. Find the attributes that survive. In step 5, read the phase and frequency correlations, then change the AGC window and read them again.
    HintAt the default ±120 ms window the phase correlates at 0.9999 before against after, and the frequency at 0.9990. Both are essentially untouched, because each already divides the trace by its own strength: multiply the trace by anything positive and the ratio is unchanged. But shorten the window and even they start to go. At ±30 ms the frequency is 0.9880, and at ±10 ms it is 0.9006 with the phase down to 0.9932. The immunity is not absolute, it holds while the gain is roughly constant across a wavelet. So the useful version of the rule is this: on data of unknown history, phase and frequency are the attributes you can still work with, provided nobody applied a short-window AGC.

The main points

  • AGC is division by the RMS amplitude. Module 03 computed the divisor; this module divides by it. There is nothing else in the process.
  • It makes the deep section visible, and that is a real and often necessary gain. On this line it turns a hundredfold difference between shallow and deep into a small one.
  • It removes exactly the differences an amplitude interpretation is looking for. A bright spot is a stretch of trace with more energy than its neighbours, which is the definition of what AGC is built to flatten.
  • Long windows are gentler. They correct the slow trend and leave local contrast alone. Short windows remove almost everything and distort the waveform as well.
  • Every trace is gained independently. After AGC, amplitudes are not comparable between traces, lines or surveys.
  • Attributes computed afterwards inherit all of it. Envelope, RMS, sweetness and impedance are all changed. Instantaneous phase and frequency are not, because the gain cancels in a ratio.
Method, sources, and what has been simplified

The data is the same synthetic line as modules 01 and 03: 96 traces of 500 samples at 2 ms, reflection coefficients convolved with a Ricker wavelet, over a low-relief anticline, with a wedge, a gas sand and its brine equivalent, interbeds and a laterally dimming reflector. The default amplitude decay is higher again here, at 14 dB per second, because AGC has nothing to do until there is a gain problem to fix. The identical model code runs in all three modules and the test harness compares their output sample by sample, so the line cannot drift between them.

The attribute is AASPI's agc as its documentation gives it: the RMS amplitude in a running window of 2K+1 samples, then every sample divided by it. One addition, which AASPI does not need on real data and this module does: a floor under the divisor, set at 2% of the largest RMS on the trace. A synthetic can contain a genuinely silent stretch where σ goes to zero and the division goes to infinity; real data always has noise in it. Where the floor takes over, the module says so rather than drawing the result as though it meant something.

Window lengths. The AASPI default is ±500 ms, which on a one-second synthetic would be the entire trace, so the slider here runs from ±10 to ±300 ms with a default of ±120. The documentation's own advice is the important part and it is stated plainly there: long-window AGC is safer than short-window AGC for preserving relative amplitudes, and trace-to-trace AGC is a poor choice before true amplitude analysis.

The measurements. Contrast between the gas and brine sands is the peak absolute amplitude of the sand event on trace 66 against trace 20, read within a window around the sand that follows the structure. Waveform preservation is the correlation between the shallow marker's waveform before and after AGC, over a window of ±40 ms centered on it, with each version scaled to its own peak so that the correlation measures shape rather than size. The step 5 comparisons recompute the envelope, instantaneous phase and instantaneous frequency from scratch on both versions of the trace, using the same library module 01 uses.

Simplifications. One dimension, normal incidence, acoustic, no multiples and no transmission losses. The decay is a gain function in dB per second rather than a model of spherical divergence and absorption, so the shape of the fall-off is a stand-in. Real AGC implementations differ in details this module does not cover: some use a mean absolute value rather than an RMS, some taper the window, some apply the gain in overlapping blocks rather than sample by sample, and some clip the gain. All of them share the property this module is about.
