Module 03  ·  Single-trace attributes

RMS amplitude: how loud is it around here

Modules 01 and 02 asked questions about a single instant. This one asks about a stretch of time. Slide a window down the trace, and at every sample report the average size of what is inside it. The arithmetic takes one line. The window length takes judgement, and it turns out to matter more than almost anything in the geology.

1A window, slid down the trace

Square, average, square root

Put a box around the cursor. Take every sample inside it, square each one, average the squares, and take the square root of the answer. That number is the RMS amplitude at the cursor. Move the box down one sample and do it again:

RMS = √( average of d² inside the window )

The squaring is doing two jobs. It makes everything positive, so a trough counts as much as a peak and the answer never cancels itself out. And it weights the big samples much more heavily than the small ones, so RMS is really a measure of energy, not of average size.

The shaded box on the left panel is the window at the cursor. Drag the cursor, or the half-length slider in the header, and watch the box and the number change together.

Compare the last two numbers. RMS is always smaller than the largest sample in the window and always bigger than zero, no matter where the cursor sits. It cannot be negative, it has no phase and it has no polarity: a peak and a trough of the same size give exactly the same answer. That is useful when you want to know how much is happening, and useless when you want to know what.

2The window length is the attribute

Same trace, three different answers

Nothing about the data changes between the three curves below. Only the length of the box does, and the three answers are not versions of one measurement — they are measurements of different things.

  • A short window, a fraction of a wavelet, tracks each event individually. It rises and falls with the wiggle and looks a great deal like the envelope from module 01. That is not a coincidence: for a single event the two are the same shape, and the RMS reads about — of the envelope, because a sine wave's RMS is its peak over √2.
  • A window of a few wavelets stops resolving events and starts measuring packages. This is the setting most interval attribute maps are made with.
  • A long window flattens the whole trace into a slow trend. Useful for gain and for comparing one part of a survey against another, useless for anything stratigraphic.

There is no correct choice here, only a choice that matches your question. What is not allowed is picking one and forgetting you picked it, because the map you produce is a map of your window as much as of the rock.

If you have both attributes in a volume and cannot see why they look so alike, this is why. It also means running both and calling them independent evidence is a mistake.

3What it looks like on a section

Where RMS is genuinely easier to read

On the amplitude section you are looking at lines, and your eye has to judge how bright each one is against the ones around it. On the RMS section you are looking at zones. The gas sand on the right becomes a blob you can outline with a pencil, which is exactly the job most people are doing when they reach for this attribute.

In real work this is usually run between two horizons rather than over a fixed window, producing one number per trace and a map rather than a section. The arithmetic is the same; the window is just cut to follow the geology instead of the clock.

Look carefully at the vertical size of the bright zone in each display, though. On the amplitude section the sand response is — tall. On the RMS section the same feature is — tall. Nothing grew. The window did that, and step 4 is about how.

The two ratios are close but not equal, and which is larger depends on the window. RMS is an energy average over a stretch of time, so it rewards a feature that is bright and long over one that is brighter but brief. It is not a neutral summary of amplitude. A thin, very bright bed can lose to a thicker, duller one.

4Bright events smear

The anomaly is taller than the thing that made it

A window centered anywhere within half a window-length of a bright event still contains that event, so it still reports a high value. The consequence is unavoidable: an RMS anomaly is taller than the feature that made it, and it grows with the window. The sand here is 50 ms thick and never changes. Measured where the anomaly falls to half its peak, it comes out 24 ms tall at ±4 ms, 106 ms at ±20, and 540 ms at ±80 — ten times the sand, from a window four times longer than the sand itself.

The panel below reads the RMS at a fixed time — above the top of the gas sand, where there is no sand and nothing bright at all. Watch that number as you change the window length in the header.

This is also why an interval RMS map straddling a strong reflector is so often disappointing. If the window catches the same bright event everywhere, the map is flat, and the flat map is the window talking rather than the geology.

The last readout measures how tall the RMS anomaly around the sand is, taken where it falls to half its peak. Compare it against the sand, which is 50 ms thick whatever the slider says. The growth is not a simple sum — at a long window the value never falls back far enough to register an edge at all, so the anomaly runs away — but the direction is reliable, and the useful habit is this: whatever you are looking at on an RMS display is taller than the thing that made it, and you cannot tell by how much unless you know the window.

5RMS never reaches zero

In a quiet zone you are measuring your own noise

Squaring makes everything positive, so nothing in the window can cancel anything else out. Signal and noise both contribute, and they combine the way the sides of a right triangle do:

RMS² = signal² + noise²

Two things follow. In a loud zone the noise is almost invisible, because adding a small number in quadrature to a large one changes it very little. In a quiet zone there is nothing else, so the RMS value is your noise level and nothing more. The attribute cannot tell you it has run out of signal. It just keeps returning a positive number.

The readouts below check the arithmetic live, on the quiet stretch of this trace. The model is computed twice, once with the noise and once without, so the signal and the noise can be measured separately and then compared against what the triangle predicts. This is the reason RMS values cannot be compared between two surveys, or between two vintages of the same survey, without knowing what the noise is doing in each. A quiet area that looks less prospective may simply have been shot better. It is also why an RMS map made below the deepest good data will look perfectly convincing and mean nothing at all.

6Where this attribute actually gets used

One line is not a survey

Everything so far has been a section, because that is how you learn what an attribute does. It is not how anyone uses this one. An RMS amplitude is nearly always extracted in a window that follows a horizon, one number per trace, and displayed as a map. That is the deliverable, and it changes what you can see.

The line you have been working on is one crossline out of a small volume. Below on the left is a map of RMS amplitude in a window below the top of the sand. On the right is a section through the volume at whichever crossline you choose, with the extraction window drawn on it.

Set the window top to about 80 ms and its length to 40 ms, and a channel appears. It has been in the data the whole time. It is — crosslines away from the line every other step in this module has been showing, so no section you have looked at has come anywhere near it. That is the case for map view in one picture: a section tells you about a line, and geology is not arranged in lines.

Move the window top back to 0 ms and the channel vanishes, replaced by the sand. Both maps are correct; they are maps of different intervals, and neither of them announces which. This is the single most common way an amplitude map goes wrong — not bad arithmetic, but a window sitting 20 ms from where the interpreter thought it was. On a map with no section beside it there is nothing to catch that.

?Why this one is everywhere

The most-used attribute in interpretation, and the most abused

If you only ever see one attribute map in your career it will be an RMS amplitude map. It is on every system, it takes seconds, and the picture it makes is easy to explain to someone who does not do this for a living. All of that is true, and none of it makes the map mean what people usually take it to mean.

What it is used for

  • Interval amplitude maps. A window between two horizons, one number per trace, contoured. The standard first-pass look at where a reservoir interval is bright.
  • Direct hydrocarbon indicators. Bright spots, flat spots and gas-charged sands, screened quickly before anything more careful.
  • Stratigraphic shapes. Channels, fans and bars often show as coherent bright zones in an interval, and the map reads as a picture of the depositional system.
  • Quality control. An RMS section is a fast way to see gain problems, acquisition footprint — a pattern left in the data by the shape of the survey rather than by the geology — and where the data stops being usable with depth.
  • As the divisor in AGC, which is the whole of module 04.

The four ways it will mislead you

The window is an interpretation. Not a setting, not a default. A short window maps events and a long one maps packages, and the same data honestly gives both. Whatever you chose has to travel with the map, because nothing in the picture says what it was.

Everything is taller than it looks. A 50 ms sand produces an anomaly of 106 ms at a ±20 ms window and 540 ms at ±80 ms, without changing at all. Two thin beds closer together than the window merge into one, and no amount of looking at the display will separate them.

It has no floor and no polarity. RMS in an empty zone returns your noise level, and it cannot distinguish a hard bed from a soft one. A bright RMS anomaly says something is happening, not what.

It is only as good as the amplitudes going in. Any gain applied upstream changes it everywhere. This is the trap module 04 is entirely about: RMS of AGC'd data is close to constant by construction, because AGC is defined as dividing by exactly this number.

The habit worth building: before believing an amplitude map, find out the window length, the processing vintage, and whether any gain was applied. If nobody can tell you all three, the map is a picture rather than a measurement.

Exercises

  1. Find the factor between RMS and the envelope. In step 2, read the ratio and the correlation between the short-window RMS and the envelope from module 01.
    HintOn the default trace the short-window RMS is 0.689 of the envelope, with a correlation of 0.957 between their shapes. The number to compare that against is 1/√2 = 0.707, which is the ratio for a pure sine wave: RMS is the peak divided by root two. The agreement is not exact because a seismic trace is not a sine wave and the window covers a bit more than one cycle, and it drifts when you change the frequency — 0.727 at 40 Hz, 0.681 at 15 Hz, because the same 6 ms window covers a different fraction of a cycle each time. The exact number matters less than what it means: a short-window RMS and an envelope are two calculations of nearly the same thing, so having both in a volume does not give you two pieces of evidence.
  2. Measure the smear. In step 4, read the RMS at the fixed point above the sand with the window at ±8 ms, then at ±40 ms, then at ±80 ms.
    HintThe point being measured is 60 ms above the top of the sand, where the model has no reflector at all and the trace is nearly flat. At ±8 ms the RMS reads 0.005, which is honest. At ±20 it reads 0.026. At ±40, 0.104. At ±60, 0.178. At ±80, 0.264. Nothing was ever there. Every one of those numbers is the sand leaking upward into a window long enough to reach it, and on a map that leak is indistinguishable from a real anomaly at that level. This is the mechanism behind almost every disappointing interval amplitude map: the window caught something bright just outside the interval.
  3. Grow an anomaly without touching the geology. In step 4, watch the height of the RMS anomaly around the sand as you take the window from ±4 to ±80 ms.
    HintThe sand stays 50 ms thick throughout, and the envelope anomaly stays 50 ms with it. The RMS anomaly does not: 24 ms at ±4, 30 ms at ±8, 106 ms at ±20, 138 ms at ±40, 328 ms at ±60 and 540 ms at ±80. At the short end it is narrower than the sand, because a short window resolves the individual peaks and troughs inside the event and the half-height crossing lands between them. At the long end it runs away, because the RMS never falls far enough between features to register an edge at all. Neither extreme is measuring the sand. Somewhere in the middle the number is useful, and nothing in the display tells you where that is.
  4. Watch the noise floor appear. In step 5, put the noise at 0, then 20%, then 40%, reading the measured RMS and the prediction each time.
    HintIn the quiet window at 828 ms the signal on its own reads 0.0088 and never changes. The noise reads 0.0000, 0.0100, 0.0200, 0.0300, 0.0400 as you turn the slider. The measured RMS goes 0.0088, 0.0139, 0.0225, 0.0320, 0.0417, and the triangle predicts 0.0088, 0.0133, 0.0219, 0.0313, 0.0409 — within about 3% every time, the residual being the cross term between signal and noise that only vanishes over an infinitely long window. Two things to take away. The attribute is measuring the noise almost entirely by 20%, and it looks exactly as respectable doing that as it does over the sand. And the value it reports can be pushed anywhere you like by reprocessing, without a single rock moving.
  5. Separate two sands that are not separate. In step 3, compare the gas and brine ratios on RMS and on raw amplitude, at a short window and a long one.
    HintThe raw amplitude ratio between the gas sand and the brine sand is ×3.3 at every window, because the window has nothing to do with it. The RMS ratio is ×3.6 at ±4 ms, ×3.9 at ±20, ×3.8 at ±40 and ×3.5 at ±80. It peaks in the middle. A short window resolves the individual lobes and loses some of the contrast between them; a long window dilutes the sand with the quiet rock above and below it. Between those, the window is roughly the length of the feature and the contrast is best. That is a real and useful effect, and it is also a warning: the number you report depends on a setting you chose, so the ratio is not a property of the rocks.
  6. Find something no section showed you. In step 6, walk the extraction window down from 0 ms below the horizon to 110 ms, in 20 ms steps, watching the map and the contrast readout.
    HintThe contrast between the channel and the rock beside it goes ×0.8 at 0 to 60 ms, ×1.3 at 40 to 80, ×2.7 at 60 to 100, ×3.7 at 70 to 110, ×4.5 at 80 to 120, and then back down to ×2.4 at 90 to 130. The channel is 78 ms below the horizon and 30 ms thick, so the best window is the one sitting on it and the worst are the ones that are not. Look at the first number: below ×1, meaning the channel reads slightly darker than its surroundings at that window. You are mapping the gas lobe there and the channel is not in the picture at all. Two things follow. Every map you make is a map of an interval, and that interval is a decision someone made. And a map that shows nothing may be a map of the wrong 40 milliseconds rather than a map of nothing, which is why the section beside it is not optional.
  7. One bright RMS map, four causes. An interval RMS map shows a bright zone across part of your survey. Name at least three things that could produce it, only one of which is a change in the rock, and say how you would test each.
    HintGenuinely higher reflection coefficients — a gas sand, a hard streak, a change in lithology — is the answer everyone reaches for. Tuning is the second: step 3 showed the gas and brine sands separated by ×3.9 on RMS but the wedge on this same line changes its envelope by a factor of nearly five with the reflection coefficients held fixed, purely through interference. The third is the window: step 4 showed a point with nothing in it reading 0.005 at ±8 ms and 0.264 at ±80 ms, because a long window reaches a bright event 60 ms away. The fourth is processing gain, which module 04 is about, and it can brighten a zone without anything in the ground changing at all. Testing them: shorten the window and see whether the anomaly survives, which separates the window effect from the rest. Look at the interval thickness, since a tuning brightness tracks thickness and a fluid effect does not. Check the amplitude section underneath the map, since a real contrast has a coherent event and a gain artifact often does not. And find out what was done to the amplitudes before you got them.

The main points

  • RMS is an energy average over a window. Square, average, square root. Always positive, never zero, no polarity and no phase.
  • The window length decides what the attribute measures. Short windows map events, long windows map packages, and the same trace gives both honestly.
  • A short-window RMS is very nearly the envelope, scaled by about 1/√2. They are not independent measurements of anything.
  • Every anomaly is at least a window taller than the feature that made it. Bright events smear into their neighborhood, and nearby beds merge.
  • In a quiet zone RMS reports your noise. Signal and noise add in quadrature, so the attribute has a floor it can never go below and never announces.
  • It inherits every gain decision made upstream. Which makes it powerful, cheap, and the easiest attribute in the set to misread.
  • It is a map attribute. Sections teach you what it does; interval maps are what it is for. A feature that no section happens to cross stays invisible until somebody makes one.
Method, sources, and what has been simplified

The data is the same synthetic line as module 01, deliberately: the wedge, the gas sand and its brine equivalent, the interbeds and the reflector that dims are all in the same places, so a reader who has met them once does not have to meet a new section here. 96 traces of 500 samples at 2 ms, reflection coefficients convolved with a Ricker wavelet, an amplitude decay in dB per second, and band-limited noise expressed as a percentage of the section's RMS. The one difference from module 01 is the default decay, which is set higher here because gain with depth is what makes this attribute and the next one worth having.

The attribute is AASPI's rms_amplitude as its documentation gives it: a boxcar window of 2K+1 samples centered on each sample, squared, averaged, square-rooted. The window is shortened rather than padded at the ends of the trace, so the first and last samples are not biased toward zero. AASPI notes that a long window is better computed with an add-and-drop running sum in double precision; this implementation is the plain form, which is slower and easier to read, and gives the same answer on a trace this short.

A note on the name. The AASPI documentation calls this quantity the standard deviation of the data in the window, and then gives the formula for the root mean square, which does not remove the mean. On seismic data the two agree closely, because a trace has almost no mean. They are not the same quantity, and they separate as soon as anything puts a bias in the data. Both are implemented in this site's shared library so the difference can be looked at rather than argued about; the displays here use the RMS form, which is what the equation says and what the program computes.

The noise-floor demonstration in step 5 computes the model twice, once with noise and once without, and measures the signal and the noise separately in the same window. That is a luxury only a synthetic allows, and it is the point: on real data you can never separate the two, which is exactly why the floor is invisible. The quiet window is found by searching the noise-free trace for the 60 ms window with the least energy in it, so it moves when the geology moves.

The volume, for step 6. The first five steps use one line. Step 6 needs a map, so the model is extended to 48 crosslines — and extended so that crossline 12 is exactly the line the other steps show. The cross-dip is zero there and the channel never comes closer than six crosslines, so nothing measured in steps 1 to 5 changes; the harness checks that the volume and the line agree sample for sample. The channel is a soft, meandering fill about 30 ms thick sitting 78 ms below the top of the sand. The horizon used for the extraction is the top of the sand taken straight from the model rather than picked, so it carries no picking error — on real data a horizon wanders a few milliseconds and the window wanders with it, which is one more reason a map and a section belong side by side. To keep the volume quick to build, only the times a horizon window can reach are computed, and the band-limited noise field is reused across crosslines with an offset rather than generated independently for each.

Simplifications. One dimension for the first five steps, normal incidence, acoustic, no multiples and no transmission losses. The decay is applied as a gain function rather than modeled from spherical divergence and absorption, so it is a stand-in for the real thing rather than a simulation of it. AASPI runs on a 3D volume and can take its window between interpreted horizons, which is how most interval amplitude maps are actually made; there are no horizons here, so the window is a fixed length in time.
